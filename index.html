Incident Response Leadership

IR Project Leader — Computer Security Incident Response Team

cross-functional team · full incident response plan

I served as the incident response project leader for my capstone team, coordinating the development of a full Incident Response Plan and Procedures document for Cyber Tree Systems. My job was to keep the plan coherent as it grew: running working sessions, folding in feedback across revisions, and making sure every section lined up before submission.

The final plan covered the full incident lifecycle with a set of response playbooks, a CSIRT structure with clear roles and escalation paths, pre-approved emergency actions, a dedicated ransom-response procedure, and quick-reference contact directories for use during an active incident. The leadership challenge was less technical than organizational: turning the input of a six-person team into one document that reads with a single voice and actually works under pressure.

Threat Intelligence — Salt Typhoon Breach Analysis

I wrote an individual threat intelligence report on Salt Typhoon, the Chinese state-sponsored campaign that breached at least nine U.S. telecommunications carriers and went undetected for over a year. I built the analysis from open-source reporting and structured it around the Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain, mapping the adversary's actions, capabilities, and infrastructure across every phase, then laying out courses of action to discover, detect, deny, and disrupt the threat.

The findings that stood out: the attackers spread to more than 100,000 routers inside a single carrier after compromising one privileged management account that had no multi-factor authentication, and they reached the CALEA lawful-intercept systems, exposing which of its own operatives the U.S. had under surveillance. The recommendations I drew from it are unglamorous and exactly the point: enforce MFA on privileged accounts, patch internet-facing edge devices on a strict timeline, segment management networks, and audit access to the most sensitive systems.

Threat intelligence is point-in-time. This analysis reflects open-source reporting as of March 2026; the campaign's confirmed scope has since grown.

Vulnerability Assessment & Server Hardening

Two sides of the same job: finding what is wrong with a system, and locking down a system so there is nothing to find. I ran a full vulnerability assessment against a hidden server, then hardened my own Windows and Linux servers down to only the services they needed.

Vulnerability Assessment of a Hidden Server

I was tasked with finding and assessing a hidden server on the network. I used netdiscover for passive monitoring and nmap for active scanning, and that surfaced a hidden Linux host at an internal lab address. Once I had it, I ran a Greenbone scan against it to pull a complete picture of its weaknesses.

Greenbone returned 471 results. That is noise, not a report, so I filtered it down: I dropped Log, Debug, and False Positive levels, threw out anything scoring below 70 on Quality of Detection, and left out everything classified as Low per the scope. That brought it to 44 real findings I could stand behind. I grouped them by severity and wrote the High issues up first, because those are the ones that get someone owned today.

I also caught two hidden Windows servers in netdiscover and nmap, but they dropped off the network before I could get Greenbone onto them. I grabbed the nmap output while they were up and flagged them for follow-up scans.

Server Hardening — Windows & Linux

The flip side of the assessment. Across the term I stood up and locked down a Windows Server 2022 host and an Ubuntu host, exposing only the required services (HTTPS, secure mail over SMTPS/IMAPS/POP3S, DNS, SSH, and PostgreSQL) and proving the state with scans from a separate Kali box. The goal every module was the same: nmap from the outside should show only what is supposed to be there, and nothing else.

Default-Deny Firewall

iptables · Linux host
  • INPUT chain policy set to DROP, so anything not explicitly allowed is blocked
  • Explicit ACCEPT rules for only the required ports: 443, 465, 993, 995, 53, 22, 5432
  • Established/related traffic allowed back through
  • Verified the live ruleset with iptables -L -v -n

AppArmor · Linux host
